Boston · Walk-Network Accessibility

How much of Boston is a fifteen-minute walk away?

For every corner of Boston's real walking network, this map asks a simple question: within a fifteen-minute walk along actual streets and sidewalks — not a straight-line radius — how many of four daily needs can you reach? Groceries, healthcare, education, and recreation, pulled fresh from OpenStreetMap and routed across a 222,000-node street graph built for this piece. Cranberry hexagons reach fewer than half; harbor navy reaches all four. Hover any hexagon for its average walk times.

Method & sources

Inspired by Milan Janosov's "How Walkable Is Your City? Analyzing Urban Access with Python & OSM" (#30DayMapChallenge, Day 14/30 — OpenStreetMap), which measured 15-minute-city access in Zurich, Vienna, Budapest, and London by routing across real OSM street graphs with the city2graph package. This piece applies the same idea to Boston, rebuilt independently: Boston's administrative boundary (OSM relation 2315704) was used to pull every walkable way inside city limits via the Overpass API — 56,842 ways, 243,581 nodes — filtered to exclude motorways and no-foot-access links. The largest connected component (222,292 nodes) became a weighted graph in NetworkX, with edge weights as real haversine distances along each street segment.

Four daily-need categories were pulled the same way: groceries (shop=supermarket/convenience/grocery/greengrocer, 254 locations), healthcare (amenity=hospital/clinic/doctors/pharmacy, 127), education (amenity=school/kindergarten/college, 312), and recreation (leisure=fitness_centre/sports_centre/park/playground plus amenity=gym, 837 — Boston's park system dominates this count). Each POI was snapped to its nearest graph node, then a multi-source Dijkstra search from every category's node set found the true network-distance to the nearest example of that need for every one of the 222,292 nodes at once. A point is "reached" within a walking cutoff of 1,200 network-meters, roughly 15 minutes at a 80 m/min (4.8 km/h) walking pace — the same rule-of-thumb pace used in most 15-minute-city research.

Rendering all 222,292 nodes would overwhelm both the browser and the file size, so the analysis keeps 21,499 sample points: street intersections and dead-ends (degree ≠ 2, dropping the mid-block vertices that just trace a curve), thinned to at most one point per 45-meter cell, keeping the highest-scoring node where several competed for a cell. Those points are then binned into a 3,559-cell hexagon grid (~200 m across, only cells containing at least one point are drawn), and each hexagon shows the rounded average score and mean walk times of the points inside it — so parks, water and rail yards with no street nodes stay blank rather than being filled with guesses. This changes the map's density, not its geography — a five-way intersection downtown and a cul-de-sac in West Roxbury each still get exactly the accessibility score their real position on the network earned. The lowest-scoring pockets on the map are real too: the Franklin Park and Arnold Arboretum interiors (green, but far from groceries or clinics), the industrial waterfront edges of East Boston and South Boston near Logan Airport and the Reserved Channel, and the lower-density blocks of West Roxbury and Hyde Park toward the city line.

Sources: OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL 1.0 (street network and points of interest, via the Overpass API) · Nominatim (Boston administrative boundary) · basemap and rendering by Mapbox GL JS. Data pulled 2026-09-16; OpenStreetMap changes continuously, so a re-run on a different day will differ slightly.
