Twin Cities of the Border

Fifteen sister-city pairs along the US–Mexico border — the actual twin communities that grew up face-to-face across the line. Every city is drawn as its own real municipal boundary1, blue for the US side and green for the Mexican side — the actual outline of the city, not a stand-in shape — so San Diego and Tijuana's real footprints visibly overlap, while Columbus and Puerto Palomas barely touch as two tiny towns. In-person cross-border commuting is overwhelmingly local, not long-distance, so each Mexican city's paired US twin shown here also doubles as its most likely destination for anyone who lives in Mexico and works in the US. Click a city to fly in and see the real road connecting the pair.

1 Every city's shape is its real administrative boundary, pulled from OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API: US cities are the official city boundary (admin_level 8); Mexican cities are the municipio boundary (admin_level 6, Mexico's closest equivalent to a US city+county unit — the same unit the population figures below use). Each boundary is simplified for file size (Douglas-Peucker, to roughly 20-90 points) and only its largest ring is kept, dropping small holes or exclaves. All thirty cities in this map resolved to a real OSM boundary; a population-scaled circle (radius √population/100 km) exists in the code only as a defensive fallback for the rare place OSM has no boundary relation for, and isn't currently used anywhere on the map. One matching hazard worth naming: OpenStreetMap tags US counties at the same admin_level (6) as Mexican municipios, so an early pass of this fetch briefly (and wrongly) matched four Mexican cities to their nearest US county across the line — Ciudad Juárez to El Paso County, for instance. Every match was re-verified against the relation's own tags and refetched with US entities explicitly excluded, so what's shown here are the real Mexican municipios. Population figures mix sourcing by necessity: San Diego, El Paso, McAllen, Brownsville and Laredo use U.S. Census Bureau metropolitan-statistical-area estimates, since those metros extend well past the border-adjacent core; smaller US border towns use city-proper population. Tijuana, Ciudad Juárez, Mexicali, Nuevo Laredo, Reynosa and Matamoros are themselves large, border-oriented cities, so metro-area estimates apply directly; smaller Mexican border towns use municipality population. The smallest pairs — Columbus–Puerto Palomas, Presidio–Ojinaga, Rio Grande City–Camargo, Progreso–Nuevo Progreso — rely on more approximate population figures where precise recent counts are harder to source; treat those as illustrative, not precise. There is no publicly published dataset of cross-border commute flows at the city-pair level — real academic estimates exist only patchily, mainly for San Diego–Tijuana, and vary by study and definition — so the "most likely US work city" claim reflects the well-documented reality in border-studies research that in-person cross-border commuting is overwhelmingly local, rather than a specific measured percentage. The road connecting each pair is a real driving route from the Mapbox Directions API, not a straight line.

Sources: OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL 1.0 (city boundaries, via Overpass API) · U.S. Census Bureau, Population and Housing Unit Estimates · Macrotrends, Mexico metro-area population series (UN-based)
