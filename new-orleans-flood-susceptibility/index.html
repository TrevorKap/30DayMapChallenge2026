Where New Orleans holds water

Much of New Orleans sits below sea level, so where rain pools and where surge arrives depends on a few feet of ground. This index combines bare-earth elevation, local low spots, distance to surge water and paved surface on a 20 m grid. The pale ribbon along the Mississippi is the natural-levee ground that early settlers built on; the dark blocks along the lakefront are the drained polders. Method & sources

Each 20 m cell in 29.85–30.09°N, 90.20–89.85°W gets four factor scores from 0 (safest) to 1 (most susceptible), combined as a weighted sum: elevation (USGS 3DEP bare-earth DEM, NAVD88; +3 m scores 0, −2 m and below scores 1); local depression (how far the cell sits below the mean ground level within about 1.2 km, capped at 1 m, which captures bowls where rain pools); distance to surge water (straight-line distance to Lake Pontchartrain, the Lake Borgne marshes and the navigation canals, falling from 1 at the shoreline to 0 at 4 km); and imperviousness (NLCD 2021 percent impervious surface, a runoff proxy). The summed score is stretched between its 2nd and 98th percentiles for color. The panel percentile is the share of land in the study area that scores lower.

Choices and limits: the Mississippi is deliberately left out of the distance factor, because it runs above the city on its own natural levee and the surge threat comes from the lake side. Open water is taken from NLCD's 30 m land cover, so narrow drainage canals are mostly missed. The index does not model levees, floodwalls, pump capacity, rainfall intensity, storm surge height or land subsidence, and the weights are judgment calls rather than fitted to observed flood extents. The neighborhood table is a sanity check, not a calibration: natural-levee ground (French Quarter, Garden District, Uptown) should rank well below the old backswamp (Lakeview, Gentilly, Lower Ninth Ward), and the build script fails if it does not. Read it as a screening map of where water is most likely to collect or arrive, not a flood-insurance or evacuation product.
