How to read this

Each cell is a stylized pixel in an illustrative NDVI‑anomaly raster: it does not encode raw satellite reflectance, but a composite pattern built to match the well‑documented geography of El Niño vegetation stress in this region — heaviest on the peat and monsoon belts, lighter toward the equatorial fringe. Brown reads as deficit, pale straw as near‑normal, muted green as slightly above normal.

Real operational versions of this product exist: agencies such as USGS/FEWS NET and national meteorological services publish eMODIS or VIIRS NDVI‑anomaly composites for exactly this kind of early drought signal, months ahead of crop or fire reporting.

The mechanism

During El Niño, the Walker circulation's rising branch shifts east, away from the Indo‑Pacific, weakening the convection that normally waters Indonesia, Papua New Guinea, northern and eastern Australia, and the Mekong and Chao Phraya basins. The rainfall deficit reaches vegetation within weeks to months, first as reduced canopy moisture, then as measurable NDVI decline.

The 2015–16 El Niño coincided with severe peat and forest fires across Sumatra and Kalimantan, a frost‑and‑drought food‑security crisis in the PNG highlands, Thailand's worst drought in two decades, and record saltwater intrusion into Vietnam's Mekong Delta rice paddies; the 2019 and 2023–24 episodes produced comparable, if less extreme, stress across the same belt.
