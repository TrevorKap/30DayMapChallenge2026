The Climate Grid

Two variables, one surface: temperature and precipitation, each split into thirds and crossed into a nine-cell palette. Where the color runs dark and saturated, a place is both hot and wet, or cold and dry — the blend tells the story a single-hue map can't.

Reading the map

Every cell on land is classified twice — once by its 30‑year average temperature, once by its average annual rainfall — each split into terciles (driest/coldest third, middle third, wettest/hottest third) across the whole grid. The two class numbers pick one of nine colors from a blended palette: a warm rust ramp for temperature crossed with a cool teal ramp for precipitation, combined with a multiplicative blend so double-high cells (hot & wet) read dark and saturated while double-low cells (cold & dry) stay pale. The technique follows Philippe Rivière's bivariate-map notebook for Observable Plot — quantile classification, a barycentric-style raster, and a blended two-ramp legend — reimplemented here in plain Canvas + D3 for a static page.

Where this comes from

Climate normals are 20‑year averages (2001–2020) of 2‑meter air temperature and corrected total precipitation, from NASA's POWER project (MERRA‑2 reanalysis), sampled on its native 0.5°×0.625° grid across a box from 5°N–84°N and 170°W–10°W (extended east and north to cover Greenland) — roughly 41,000 grid cells, fetched tile by tile from the public POWER Climatology API. Coastlines and country borders are Natural Earth 1:50m via world-atlas.

How it's drawn

A Lambert conformal conic projection (standard parallels 20°N/60°N, central meridian 96°W) frames the grid — the same "North America" preset used by USGS, NOAA, and Esri (EPSG:102009). Every pixel inside the map is inverse-projected back to a longitude/latitude, bilinearly sampled from the temperature and precipitation grids, classified, and colored — then the whole raster is clipped to the land outline in a single canvas pass.
