The River That Runs Dry

The Colorado once ran wild from the Rockies to the Gulf of California, carrying spring snowmelt through a wide desert delta. Over the last century, dams and canals turned it into one of the most heavily managed rivers in the world, split among seven states, Mexico, and millions of acres of farmland. Now Lake Mead and Lake Powell sit at record lows, and the river runs dry before it reaches the sea in most years.

About this map

The river network, basin outline, and reservoir shapes are traced from real hydrography, not invented geometry. The Colorado, Green, Gila, and San Juan mainstems come from Natural Earth's rivers layer. The Gunnison, Little Colorado, Virgin, Salt, and Verde come from OpenStreetMap waterway relations. The basin boundary is the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset's Upper and Lower Colorado regions (HUC 14 and 15). Lake Mead, Lake Powell, and Lake Havasu are traced from Natural Earth's lakes layer, and the national park boundaries come from the NPS Land Resources Division.

Color combines two qualitative reads at each reach: water stress (demand pressure from agriculture, cities, and interstate allocation) and water level (how the reach's flow or reservoir storage compares with its historic norm). Both come from the reporting cited below, not a live per-reach gauge feed. Line width scales with each reach's typical flow, labeled in cubic feet per second in the legend.
