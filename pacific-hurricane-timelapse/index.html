The Storm Lane

Three illustrative hurricanes move through the eastern Pacific over a modeled six-day window, built to show what they actually do to shipping underneath them. The teal vessels sail six real trade lanes — real ports, real great-circle geometry, real distances — picked because they genuinely cross this stretch of ocean at a modeled ~20-knot cruise speed2. A ship doesn't just skirt a storm: once it's within a hurricane's reach it turns away, slows to a crawl, and rides it out — turning coral — before resuming its course once the storm has moved on. Storm track color follows the real NHC Saffir–Simpson scale, from tropical storm green through Category 5 magenta. Each storm's translucent ring is a modeled hazard buffer that grows and shrinks continuously with wind speed1; the small dashed core inside it is a genuine physical estimate — radius of maximum wind, from the Willoughby et al. (2006) formula — not an NHC forecast cone.

1 Storm tracks: three named systems ("Talon", "Reya", "Odessa") moving through the eastern Pacific over a modeled 6-day window built to follow typical Eastern Pacific hurricane behavior — westward-then-recurving tracks, realistic intensification/weakening curves, and the real NHC Saffir–Simpson wind-speed thresholds and color convention. This is an illustrative composite, not a transcription of any specific NOAA/National Hurricane Center advisory, best-track, or forecast-cone product. The translucent outer ring is a modeled hazard buffer whose radius is smoothly interpolated from wind speed (not stepped by category), so it grows and shrinks continuously across the timelapse. The small dashed inner ring is a genuinely real, cited estimate of the radius of maximum wind — Rmw = 51.6 · e(−0.0223·Vmax + 0.0281·|lat|) km, from Willoughby, H.E., Darling, R.W.R. & Rahn, M.E. (2006), "Parametric Representation of the Primary Hurricane Vortex, Part II," Monthly Weather Review — evaluated at each storm's own modeled wind speed and latitude at every instant.

2 Shipping lanes: six real trade routes between real ports at their real coordinates — Los Angeles/Long Beach↔Cabo San Lucas, Ensenada↔Puerto Vallarta, Los Angeles↔Manzanillo, Los Angeles↔Acajutla (El Salvador), Los Angeles↔Honolulu, and Oakland↔Honolulu. The mainland–Hawaii pair is a real, long-running trade lane (Matson Navigation has sailed it for decades); LA↔Manzanillo and LA↔Central America are real containerized trade routes too. Each lane is flown along the real great-circle path between its two ports — an approximation of the charted shipping lane, not a transcription of one — at a modeled 20-knot (≈37 km/h) cruise speed typical of container and cargo vessels, scheduled on the same compressed 6-day clock as the storms. This is the layer the piece is built around: when a ship's position comes within a hurricane's much wider "give it a wide berth" radius (about 2.2× the storm's own hazard buffer — real vessels divert well before a storm's core reaches them), it turns away from the storm's center, slows sharply, and rides out the weather before resuming its course. That reroute behavior is a modeled visualization of real maritime storm-avoidance practice, not an individual ship's actual logged track or a real captain's actual decision.
